# Win on trust, not on price

lebanon-POS cannot be the cheapest till in Lebanon, and it does not need to be. The research found Lebanese vendors publishing prices at or below the bottom of the planned range: **Table at $10 a month, YellowPOS from $15, Omega at about $30–32 a month on annual licences**, and OLX dealers selling hardware "with free software" from $199. Table and Sandooq also advertise the same headline as lebanon-POS (offline, dollars and lira, Arabic), so those three features are now the entry ticket in Lebanon and no longer a selling point. The planned pricing still holds, but only at the low end of each band: keep the free plan, launch shops and cafés at **$15 per till (about $150 a year prepaid)** and hold $20 back until there are reference customers, and price restaurants at $25–35 only if kitchen, table and QR-menu features are all included. What nobody was found advertising in Lebanon is the bundle lebanon-POS already plans: customer credit tabs (dayn) that work offline with WhatsApp reminders, owner approvals with an audit log, blind cash counts in two currencies, and a nightly WhatsApp report. Abroad, the big names give software away because card fees pay for it, then recover the subsidy with long contracts that customers hate; neither half of that model works in cash-heavy Lebanon, which leaves "public price, no contract, we never touch your money" as an honest and cheap promise. One warning applies to every number below: the researchers could not open web pages, so almost everything comes from search-engine summaries of the linked pages, coverage of some regions stopped when the search budget ran out, and every "nobody offers X" means only "we did not find it".

## How to read the evidence in this report

Every price and feature below was taken from a search-result summary of the linked page, not from the page itself. Numbers can be out of date, attached to the wrong plan, or simply wrong. Where two sources disagree, both figures are shown side by side and neither is treated as the truth. The "confidence" column in the tables uses three plain grades. **Good** means the summary attributed the figure to the vendor's own website or price list. **Fair** means a third-party listing such as Capterra, TrustRadius, OLX or a review site. **Weak** means a competitor's blog, a directory estimate, an undated figure, or a conflict nobody could resolve. Even "Good" has not been checked by a human on the real page. Many of the "best POS in Lebanon 2026" articles used as sources are written by vendors ranking themselves first (Voxire, YellowPOS, MAPOS, Sandooq), so their descriptions of rivals are biased. Dollar conversions use the researchers' own rates (fixed pegs for the Saudi riyal and UAE dirham; approximate rates for the Egyptian pound, South African rand, Nigerian naira and Turkish lira; €1 = $1.10 and £1 = $1.31).

## In Lebanon, the floor is $10 a month and "free", and the headline features are taken

Public pricing in Lebanon is no longer rare. At least six Lebanon-targeted products publish a dollar price, while the older houses (BIM POS, CeevTech, POPs LEB, IDS Libra, Wizard, Socrate, SBS, Nexel, SoftPharm) still sell on quotation. The closest rival by buyer and by price is **Table**, a Lebanon-built product that claims 300+ shops, self-serve signup, a 14-day trial, offline-first operation, dollars and lira with the rate recorded at the time of sale, Arabic, English and French, stock with recipes, kitchen tickets and a QR menu, all for $10 a month, and is built and supported by one person, a student founder ([Table](https://tablelb.com/); [Table founder page](https://tablelb.com/founder)). **Omega**, the best-known incumbent, turns out to publish fixed annual prices on its own store, which contradicts the "expensive legacy vendor" picture competitors paint of it ([Omega store](https://store.omegapos.com/products/restaurant-management-software-pos-back-office); [YellowPOS blog](https://yellow-pos.com/blog/best-pos-system-lebanon-2026)).

Three things follow. First, the real floor is not $10 but zero: Loyverse's free app, a dealer's "free software" with a $199–699 hardware purchase, and unlicensed copies, in a country whose unlicensed-software rate was 69% in 2017, the latest figure found ([Byblos Bank](https://byblosbank.com/common/economic-research-new/lebanon-this-week/lebanon-this-week-540/lebanons-piracy-rate-at-69-in-2017-37th-highest-globally-and-sixth-highest-among-arab-countries)). Second, lebanon-POS's planned $15–35 per till is mid-market: above Table and YellowPOS, level with Omega and Sandooq, well below MAPOS and the RTYLR suite. Third, no set-up, training or maintenance fee was found for any Lebanese vendor, so real first-year costs are likely higher than these list prices, and no real quote was obtained from anyone.

Two segments look closed or crowded. Pharmacies belong to **SoftPharm**, which claims 97% of Lebanese pharmacies (its own claim) and is integrated with the Health Ministry's MediTrack drug-tracking system, an integration that took eight months ([NIT](https://www.nit.com.lb/services/softpharm/); [Ministry of Public Health](https://www.moph.gov.lb/userfiles/files/News/2-9-2021-Meditrack/MediTrack%20Integration%20with%20MVC%20(1).pdf)). Restaurants are contested by BIM, Omega, MAPOS, RTYLR and ByteBite with kitchen screens, recipes, floor plans and, for RTYLR and ByteBite, claimed Toters integration ([Voxire](https://voxire.com/blog/best-pos-systems-lebanese-restaurants-2026/); [ByteBite](https://www.bytebitepos.com/)). That supports the retail-first plan. Customer voice is almost absent: only BIM POS has a real review base (4.8/5 from 46 Capterra reviews, many from 2018), where users complain that "more than 1000 reports" make it confusing, that updates force retraining, and that support is thin ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/143097/BIM-POS/reviews/)).

## Free and cheap apps are free only until a shop needs staff logins or stock control

The free app most likely to be on a Lebanese counter is **Loyverse**. Its core (till, back office, kitchen screen, customer display, loyalty) is permanently free with no contract, but its add-on prices are published in two conflicting ways, and a shop that wants staff permissions and purchase orders pays roughly $40–55 a month, more than lebanon-POS plans to charge ([Loyverse US pricing](https://loyverse.com/en-us/pricing); [Loyverse price file](https://loyverse.com/llms.txt)). Its offline mode keeps selling but, by its own help page, does not allow refunds, new customers or new items, and open tickets do not sync between devices ([Loyverse help](https://help.loyverse.com/help/offline-work-of-pos)). No Loyverse help page describes taking a second currency at checkout, and a Lebanese competitor says it has "no native dual-currency LBP handling" ([Loyverse help](https://help.loyverse.com/help/how-set-currency); [Voxire](https://voxire.pages.dev/blog/best-pos-systems-lebanese-restaurants-2026/)).

The pattern is consistent: free tiers are capped by number of products, monthly sales, a single user or a single register, and paid tiers cluster at **$10–30 per store per month**. Note the unit: most of these charge per store, not per till, so a two-till shop on lebanon-POS at $15–20 per till pays $30–40 against $10–20 per store elsewhere. A free lebanon-POS plan with one till and no cap on products or sales would match the most generous free tiers (Loyverse, Elementary, Nextar, Aronium Lite) and beat the capped ones. No data was gathered on several apps the brief named (Hike, ERPLY, iReap, Qasir, Moka, Pawoon, Oscar POS, Wave, Arabic-named Play Store cashier apps) because the search budget ran out.

## In the Middle East and Africa, the Gulf charges several times more and Africa gives software away

Gulf restaurant software is priced far above Lebanon. **Foodics**, the regional benchmark with over 33,500 restaurant branches, appears at roughly $60–200 a month per bundle in Saudi Arabia, though three incompatible sets of Saudi prices were found ([MENAbytes](https://www.menabytes.com/foodics-h1-numbers/)). Accounting-led Saudi products with a till add-on (Qoyod, Zoho, Daftra) sit at about $10–55. In Egypt and sub-Saharan Africa the model flips: payment companies hand out cheap terminals and earn on each card or mobile-money transaction, and software is free or an afterthought. Foodics does have some Lebanese customers, because in 2025 it shipped a Lebanon-only fix when ordinary orders in lira exceeded its transaction limit ([Foodics changelog](https://changelog.foodics.com/enhanced-support-for-high-value-orders-in-the-lebanese-market-3vkY0w)), but no Lebanese reseller or office was found, and no evidence either way was found for Rewaa, Marn or Geidea in Lebanon.

Two facts in this region matter for the "Gulf later" plan. Gulf contracts run one or two years with 8–33% discounts for prepaying, so month-to-month billing is unusual there. Jordan's rule sits awkwardly with an offline-first till and needs a legal check before any entry there. Local vendors in Jordan, Iraq and Syria were never searched.

That is how a till can be "free".

## The verdict on pricing: the plan stands, at the bottom of each band

The planned structure (a free plan, $15–20 per till for shops and cafés, $25–35 for restaurants) is defensible, but the research changes how it should be set and sold. What follows is our judgement from the prices above, not a tested result.

**Keep the free plan, and make it properly usable.** The real competition for a small Lebanese shop is free: Loyverse, a dealer's bundled software, a cracked copy, or a notebook. A subscription cannot beat "free" on price. A free tier with one till, unlimited products and sales, offline selling, dollars and lira, and a basic credit tab would match or beat every free tier found and would do more offline than Loyverse does. Two cautions come from abroad. Free ledger apps drew millions of shopkeepers in other cash-heavy markets and still mostly failed to pay for themselves: Indonesia's Lummo was liquidated and India's OkCredit was still loss-making in its 2025 financial year ([DealStreetAsia](https://media.dealstreetasia.com/stories/indonesia-lummo-liquidation-360554); [Inc42](https://inc42.com/buzz/tiger-global-backed-okcredit-narrows-fy25-loss-by-34-to-inr-23-cr/)). And lebanon-POS has no card income to subsidise free users, so the free plan must cost almost nothing to serve (data on the device, messages sent from the owner's own WhatsApp, no hands-on support). The paid plan should be justified by what others successfully charge for: cloud backup and multi-device sync, extra tills, staff accounts and approvals, longer history and richer reports, and automatic reminders ([Loyverse](https://loyverse.com/en-us/pricing); [Kyte](https://www.kyteapp.com/subscription/global)).

**Launch shops and cafés at $15, not $20.** Against Table at $10 and YellowPOS from $15, a new product with no reviews cannot credibly ask $20 for a similar headline. At $15 the gap to Table is $5 a month, which credit tabs, approvals and answered support can justify; at $20 the gap doubles. Offer about **$150 a year prepaid**, which mirrors Table's "two months free" structure and reduces monthly cash collection ([Table](https://tablelb.com/)). Move to $20 only after pilot shops are paying and renewing. Do not drop to $10: the notes show price alone cannot win against free and pirated software, and $10 leaves little to fund support, which is the main weakness of a one-person rival.

**Rethink "per till" for the second till.** Most low-cost rivals charge per store. A two-till minimarket at $15–20 per till pays $30–40, against $10 per store at Aronium, $15–20 per location at Zoho, and $39 per store at Sandooq. A simple fix is one price for the shop including the first till, with each extra till at roughly half price. This is a suggestion to test, not a finding.

**Restaurants at $25–35 work only as an all-in price.** Omega's restaurant licence is $360–380 a year, about $30–32 a month, but its QR menu costs another $100 a year, bringing the comparable total to about $38–40 a month (our arithmetic); RTYLR is "under $40" per terminal, MAPOS starts at $99 and Foodics at roughly $60 and up. So $25–35 with tables, kitchen tickets and QR menu included is competitive against the established names. It is not cheap against Table, whose $10 already includes kitchen tickets and a QR menu. Start the restaurant plan near $25 and do not sell it until those features exist.

**Expect two objections and prepare answers.** POPs LEB sells a "lifetime licence, one-time purchase", and dealers sell hardware with software thrown in ([POPs LEB](https://popsleb.com/products/pops-retail)). A one-time licence option with a yearly support fee is worth testing with pilot shops. The answer to "why pay monthly" is what free or cracked software cannot give: off-site backup, the owner's view from a phone, updates and a person who answers.

## What business models abroad teach, and which lessons fit cash-heavy Lebanon

**Payments-funded free software does not transfer.** SumUp, Zettle, Square, Revolut, Dojo, Yoco, Paymob and Mercado Pago can charge nothing for the till because they keep 1.3–3.6% of every card sale. Lebanon's shops run mostly on cash and informal credit, and the central bank caps card fees at 1.25% in sectors such as supermarkets and mini-markets ([L'Orient Today](https://today.lorientlejour.com/article/1505868/bdl-caps-merchant-fees-on-local-card-payments-at-125-in-various-sectors.html)). lebanon-POS must therefore cover support, hosting and sales from the subscription alone, should never subsidise hardware, and should never price as a percentage of sales. The compensation is that the same fact protects a modest subscription price: nobody can undercut it with card income. Two things from this world do transfer. Colombia's Siigo partnered with a bank to give shops a free QR payment option without handling the money itself, a pattern that could later fit Lebanese wallets ([La República](https://www.larepublica.co/finanzas/siigo-y-davivienda-se-unen-para-ofrecer-siigo-pos-y-qr-interoperable-gratis-a-pymes-3858683)). And payment-led firms draw constant complaints about frozen funds, such as Square's 3,131 Better Business Bureau complaints in three years, many about 90-day holds ([BBB](https://prod.bbb.org/us/ca/san-francisco/profile/credit-card-merchant-services/square-inc-1116-370609/complaints)); a software-only vendor can say plainly "we never hold your money".

**Lock-in is the most hated thing in the industry, and avoiding it is free.** Contracts exist to recover the hardware and software subsidy, and they generate the loudest complaints. In October 2026 the US Federal Trade Commission and several states sued Toast over hidden fees and hard-to-cancel subscriptions, alleging auto-renewal and a charge of $150 for each remaining month; these are allegations, not findings ([FTC](https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/10/ftc-states-take-action-against-toast-inc-over-hidden-unauthorized-fees-deceptive-subscription)). Clover resellers are reported to use non-cancellable 48-month leases ([ConsumerAffairs](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/scam_alerts/first-data-global-leasing.html?page=2)), and one Epos Now customer reported a plan jumping from £40 to £153 a month ([Trustpilot](https://uk.trustpilot.com/review/eposnow.com?page=7)). Toast's 99-cent fee added to diners' online orders, and sunday's diner fee, both ended in backlash ([Nation's Restaurant News](https://nrn.com/restaurant-segments/toast-to-remove-99-cent-fee-on-orders-over-10); [Law360](https://www.law360.com/articles/2487241)). The lesson applies fully: put "month-to-month, cancel any time, your price in writing, export your data, no fees on your customers" on the price page. Against Table and Loyverse this is only parity; against quote-only and licence vendors it is a real difference.

**Packaging ideas travel well.** Loyverse's free core with a handful of flat add-ons, an annual plan at 15–18% off instead of a contract, and a free allowance of staff seats before charging are all cheap to copy ([Loyverse](https://loyverse.com/en-us/pricing); [NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/reviews/small-business/lightspeed-retail-pos)). Flatpay grew very fast by signing shops up in person, and its best reviews name the account manager ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/04/16/flatpay-rings-up-47m-to-target-smaller-merchants-with-simple-payment-solutions)); that suits a market where owners distrust online sign-up, though it costs money that a $15 subscription recovers slowly. Vectron's single monthly fee covering till and software shows the appeal of a bundle ([Vectron](https://www.vectron-systems.com/fileadmin/Content/Unternehmen/PR-IR/News/2026/Vectron-POS_8-2026-06-22.pdf)), but lebanon-POS should leave hardware to resellers and not finance it.

**AI assistants are becoming free and expected at the top, and are not yet a reason to buy in Lebanon.** Square made its assistant generally available to US sellers in November 2026 at no extra cost, with the ability to undo its own actions on request, and Toast includes Toast IQ with its till ([Square](https://squareup.com/us/es/press/introducing-squaremates); [Toast](https://pos.toasttab.com/au/products/toast-iq)). Lightspeed launched its assistant in January 2026 ([Lightspeed](https://www.lightspeedhq.com/news/lightspeed-commerce-launches-lightspeed-ai-a-new-ai-powered-intelligence-layer-for-retail-and-hospitality/)), and in Lebanon BIM POS promotes "Bimmie" and MAPOS an "AI copilot" ([BIM POS](https://bimpos.com/gh/blog/is-your-restaurant-pos-ready-to-serve-a-global-customer-base); [MAPOS](https://www.mapos.tech/en/blog/best-restaurant-management-system-lebanon-mena-2026)). These assistants need months of data and a live connection, and nothing found shows owners keep using them. For a cash-heavy shop the equivalent that works today is the nightly WhatsApp summary of sales, cash differences, voids and overdue tabs. The lesson for later: bundle any assistant free, limit it to the owner, make its actions reversible, and never charge for it as a tier.

## What we still don't know, and what to verify first

The gaps are large, and several of them sit under the main conclusions.

No page was read in full by anyone. Every price, every feature claim and every "not found" above rests on search summaries. The vendor sites, OLX, Trustpilot and the app stores were all blocked. The first job is therefore simple and cheap: a person opens the pages and makes phone calls.

The most important unverified claim is that **no Lebanese vendor offers customer credit tabs**. It was not found in any summary; it was not confirmed absent. Table, YellowPOS, Sandooq and Omega may well have it under another name. The same applies to WhatsApp reminders, remote approvals, blind counts and photo import: absent from what was found, not proven absent. Whether Loyverse supports customer credit is contradicted between two review sites.

Real prices are unknown where they matter most. BIM POS has four incompatible published figures and no Lebanon price. CeevTech, POPs LEB, IDS Libra, Wizard, SBS, Nexel, ByteBite and SoftPharm have none. Table's price for extra terminals, YellowPOS's tiers, what Omega's hardware bundles include after year one, and what OLX "free software" actually is were all unreadable. No set-up or training fees were found for any Lebanese vendor, and no real quote was obtained. Foodics' Saudi price has three versions.

There is no customer voice. No Lebanese reviews, Reddit threads, Facebook comments or app-store ratings were retrieved for any vendor except BIM's old Capterra reviews. Nothing shows what Lebanese shop owners actually pay, what they dislike, whether they prefer a one-time licence, or how many use Table or Loyverse beyond Table's own "300+ shops".

Whole areas were never searched because the budget ran out: local vendors in Jordan, Iraq and Syria; Arabic-named Play Store cashier apps and legacy Arabic desktop packages (Al-Ameen and similar); many free apps (Hike, ERPLY, iReap, Qasir, Moka, Pawoon, Oscar, Wave); Clover's plan tiers, PAR and Oracle; European fiscal rules; offline, credit and multi-currency details for most US and European vendors; Areeba card-terminal integration and any Lebanese e-invoicing plans. Arabic and French searches returned almost nothing Lebanon-specific.

| Verify first | How | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Do Table, YellowPOS, Sandooq and Omega have customer credit, WhatsApp reminders or approvals? | Open their sites and sign up for the free trials (Table 14 days, YellowPOS one month) | Decides whether dayn is a headline or merely parity |
| What do BIM POS, POPs LEB and Omega really charge a minimarket and a café, including set-up? | Ask for three quotes as a shop owner | Sets the true price ceiling and the lifetime-licence objection |
| What is the "free software" in OLX and dealer bundles? | Call two dealers (for example Risco Tech) | This is the real floor for a shop buying hardware |
| Can Loyverse and Elementary POS handle a dollars-and-lira sale and a tab in practice? | Install both free apps and run ten test sales | They are the free alternatives owners will actually try |
| Would owners pay $15 a month, and do they prefer a one-time licence? | Ask the 10–20 pilot shops, alongside how they track dayn today | No source measures Lebanese willingness to pay |
| Does the browser app print Arabic and keep offline data safely on cheap Android tablets? | Test on real hardware | The product promise depends on it |

## Conclusion

The earlier research said the opening in Lebanon was hidden prices and missing self-serve products. This round shows that door has started to close: a one-person company already sells an offline, dual-currency, Arabic till for $10 a month with a public price and a free trial. That moves lebanon-POS's case off the features it first planned to lead with and onto the ones further down its list: the credit book, the controls that stop a till being quietly emptied, the owner's nightly message, and a support line that outlasts a single founder. Those are also the features that no payment-funded giant has reason to build, because they serve cash and informal credit, where no card fee is earned.

The second shift is about what "cheap" means. By world standards $15 is a fraction of the going rate; in Lebanon it is the middle of the market, sitting between free and a $30 incumbent with far more features. The price will be judged against the notebook and the cracked copy, so the product has to earn its fee visibly every week, and the cheapest next step is not more desk research but a week of opening rivals' pages, taking their free trials, collecting three real quotes and asking pilot shops what they would pay.
